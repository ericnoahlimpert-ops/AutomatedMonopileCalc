1. Overview

main.py loads Input.py and AdvancedSettings.py from the input folder (see the user guide). For every case (pile × soil × spring model) it:

  1. reads and validates pile.csv, soil.csv and, for TOTT_BUSWELL, the CPT file (csv_reader.py),
  2. builds the beam model in Abaqus/CAE (geometry.py),
  3. adds boundary conditions, the static step and the load (analysis.py),
  4. creates the spring model (spring_models/),
  5. applies the load levels; for every level the springs are computed, written to connector elements (connectors.py), the Abaqus job is run and evaluated, repeated until the level has converged (solver.py),
  6. writes the results of the last level (postprocessing.py, plots.py).

Each Abaqus job loads the pile from zero to the load of the level. The soil springs are nonlinear elastic, so the result does not depend on the load path, and the levels are independent jobs.

2. Pile model and mesh

  • Geometry: a wire part along the global Z axis. The pile head is at Z = 0, depths are mapped to Z = −z.
  • Elements: beam elements (ELEMENT_TYPE, default B31), one element per wire edge, so every element end node is a geometric vertex to which a soil connector can be attached.
  • Cross-section: pipe profile per prismatic segment. With DIAMETER_REFERENCE = "mid" the outer radius is D/2 + t/2, which gives A = π·D·t and I ≈ π·D³·t/8 (thin-walled, as in PISA). With SHEAR_FACTOR the transverse shear stiffness is K = κ·G·A.
  • Material: linear elastic, from the material library.
  • Conical sections: prismatic elements with the diameter and thickness at their mid-depth (stepped approximation).

Mesh. ELEMENT_COUNT elements over the whole pile. Nodes are forced at:

  • every pile section boundary,
  • the mudline,
  • every soil layer boundary.

The elements are distributed over the intervals between these depths so that the total is exactly ELEMENT_COUNT and the element lengths are as uniform as possible (every interval at least one element). With forced nodes, no element spans a change of pile or soil parameters; for a layered profile this removes an integration error of up to 5 % of the total soil resistance with 2.5 m elements (0.5 % with 0.5 m elements).

3. Soil springs

3.1 From soil reaction curves to node springs

SpringModel.compute() (spring_models/base.py):

  1. Every node gets the half element above and below it as tributary length, cut off at the mudline.
  2. Each half is evaluated separately at its own mid-depth: soil layer, σ'_v (integrated over all layers above), pile diameter, length. A node at a layer boundary therefore gets half its spring from each layer.
  3. The contributions (curve × length) of both halves are summed into one force–displacement curve [N vs m] (and moment–rotation curve [Nm vs rad] for PISA and Tott-Buswell).
  4. Base springs (PISA, Tott-Buswell) use the soil just above the pile tip.

3.2 Curve sampling

Each curve is sampled at CURVE_POINTS points: x = 0 and CURVE_POINTS − 1 logarithmically spaced points from GRID_START·x_max to x_max, plus the kinks of the model curve (e.g. y_A, y_B, y_C of Thieken). x_max is chosen per model so that the curve is fully mobilised (e.g. 1.2·y_C for Thieken, 1.2·x_u for PISA). The logarithmic spacing puts most points into the small-displacement range where the curves are most curved.

3.3 Connector elements

Every spring node is connected to ground by a wire with its own connector section (connectors.py):

ComponentBehaviour
1, 2 (U1, U2)lateral curve, nonlinear elastic, mirrored for negative displacements
4, 5 (UR1, UR2)rotational curve (PISA, Tott-Buswell)
3, 6free (axial and torsion)

Both horizontal directions use the same curve and are uncoupled. The pile tip gets a separate base connector (PISA, Tott-Buswell). For iterative models the connector sections are updated in place with the new curves.

4. Load, boundary conditions, step

  • Boundary conditions: pile tip U3 = 0 and UR3 = 0 (no vertical movement, no twist); lateral support only from the springs.
  • Load: concentrated force H at the pile head in the direction LOAD_ANGLE, plus the moment M = H·LEVER_ARM (r × F with r pointing up), so the tower does not have to be modelled.
  • Step: static, NLGEOM off, initial increment 0.1, minimum increment 1e-6, at most 1000 increments. Field output (U, UR, RF, SF, CTF, CU) only at the end of the step, or at one time point per load level when all levels run in one job.

5. Load levels and spring iteration

5.1 Non-iterative models under force control

API, Kallehave, Sørensen, PISA clay and Tott-Buswell springs do not depend on the pile response. All load levels are calculated in one Abaqus job: the load ramps up to the last level, and one output frame is written per level.

5.2 Iterative models (PISA sand, PISA with sand layers, Thieken)

The springs depend on the deflection line, so every level is solved as a fixed-point iteration. The state x_k is (v, ψ) of all nodes:

  1. Springs: compute the springs from x_k.
  2. Abaqus: run the job, giving the result F(x_k).
  3. Check: residual = max over v and ψ of max|F(x_k) − x_k| / max|F(x_k)|. The level has converged when residual ≤ ITERATION_TOLERANCE; the result then belongs exactly to the springs it was computed with.
  4. Update: otherwise relax (5.3) and go to 1. After MAX_ITERATIONS runs the level is kept and marked "NOT converged".

Starting state of a level: the deflection line of the last converged level scaled to the new load with v ~ H^a (a from the last two levels, limited to 1 … 3). A well predicted level converges after a few runs. If a level fails from the predicted state, it is retried once from the model's cold start (Thieken: SF = 1; PISA: distributed moment from p_u); if that fails too, the loading stops.

5.3 Aitken relaxation

x_{k+1} = x_k + ω_k · r_k,   r_k = F(x_k) − x_k
ω_k     = −ω_{k−1} · r_{k−1}·(r_k − r_{k−1}) / |r_k − r_{k−1}|²

r is normalised separately for v and ψ by their largest value along the pile. ω grows when the iteration converges monotonically and shrinks when it oscillates; it is limited to 0.1 … 1.0. It starts with RELAXATION, is carried over from level to level and reset after a cold start. Without a state (first run), the result itself is the next state.

6. Displacement control

With LOAD_CONTROL = "displacement" every level k has a target mudline displacement v_k = k·Δv; H is unknown and is found per level. Because the moment must stay M = H·h, the displacement is not prescribed as a boundary condition; instead H is corrected between the runs of a level:

  1. First H of the level: extrapolated linearly from the (H, v) of the last two levels; with one level through the origin; for the first level HORIZONTAL_LOAD·level factor.
  2. Run with the current H (and, for iterative models, springs from the current state).
  3. Correct H by H + K·(v_target − v):
    • Non-iterative models (fixed springs): K is the secant of the last two runs of the level (through the origin after the first run).
    • Iterative models: the springs change between the runs, so a single secant can be far off. The step converges as long as 0 < K < 2·(current tangent); a too large K oscillates or diverges, a too small K only converges more slowly. K is therefore the smallest of: secant of the last two runs, stiffness between the last two converged levels, secant through the origin of the first run.
    • The new H is kept inside the bracket of loads that gave too small / too large displacements; if the step leaves it, bisection is used. A failed job (H beyond the capacity) counts as "too large". For iterative models only the last run and failed jobs set the bracket: a bound from an older run belongs to other springs and may exclude the solution.
  4. Iterative models: the result is scaled to the target displacement (deflection line × v_target/v) before the relaxation, so the springs are always built for the target.
  5. Converged when |v − v_target|/v_target ≤ ITERATION_TOLERANCE and (for iterative models) the spring residual ≤ ITERATION_TOLERANCE.

Non-iterative models compute their springs once and only correct H (typically 4 runs per level); iterative models combine both corrections in one iteration (PISA: typically 5–13 runs per level). Displacement control remains stable close to the ultimate load, where force control fails (e.g. PISA pile C1 at v_G = D/10, see Verification).

7. Break criteria

After every converged level (force and displacement control) the criteria are checked at the mudline and at the toe:

CriterionValueInterpolated from
MAX_DISPLACEMENT|v_mudline|last two levels, or from H = 0
MAX_ROTATION|ψ_mudline| [°]last two levels, or from H = 0
VERTICAL_TANGENT_RATIO|ψ_toe| / |ψ_mudline|last two levels

The vertical tangent criterion (common in German offshore practice) requires the deflection line to have a vertical tangent at the pile toe; with growing load the deflection reaches deeper and the toe starts to rotate. For a short, stiff pile the ratio can exceed the limit already at the first level; this is reported, no load is interpolated.

When a criterion is reached, the loading stops after that level. The load and mudline displacement at the criterion are interpolated linearly in the criterion value between the last two levels. When all levels ran in one job (5.1) and the criterion is reached before the last level, the job is repeated up to that level so that the kept .odb and .cae belong to it.

8. Evaluation of the results

Mudline and toe values (pile_head.csv, criteria): v and ψ of the node at the mudline and at the tip, projected onto the direction of the largest horizontal displacement.

Section forces (pile_profile.csv) are taken from the Abaqus element output SF and SM, so they can be used to check the Abaqus results:

  • The mean over the integration points of an element is its value at mid-element.
  • Inner nodes: N, Q and M interpolated linearly between the mid-element values of the two adjacent elements.
  • Pile head and tip: only one adjacent element. N and Q are that element's values (both constant within an element, as the springs act at the nodes only); M = M_mid + Q·(z_node − z_mid) along the element (M linear, dM/dz = Q). This avoids the error of a linear extrapolation over two elements, which is distorted by the spring force at the node in between.
  • Q_above / Q_below: shear of the element above / below a node; the difference is the spring force at the node.

Plausibility check (printed after each case): M at the pile head must equal H·lever arm; Q at the head is H minus the spring force at the head node. For PISA, M jumps at every node by the moment of its rotational spring, and M at the tip is the base moment M_B.

9. Sign conventions

Load angle α from global X towards Y.

QuantityDefinition
vU1·cos α + U2·sin α — displacement in load direction
ψ−UR1·sin α + UR2·cos α — rotation in the load plane, positive when the head moves in load direction
NSF1, tension positive
QSF2·sin α − SF3·cos α, Q = +H at the pile head
MSM1·sin α + SM2·cos α, M = +H·lever arm at the pile head

The SF/SM mapping follows from the beam orientation n1 = global X (checked with loads at 0°, 45° and 90°).

10. Assumptions and limitations

  • Winkler model: uncoupled springs; soil continuity is only included through the model formulations (e.g. Thieken stretching factors, PISA moment and base springs).
  • Nonlinear elastic springs: monotonic loading only, no unloading or hysteresis, no cyclic degradation (except the API/Matlock cyclic curves).
  • No axial load and no geometric nonlinearity (NLGEOM off).
  • PISA and Thieken are calibrated for homogeneous sand; layered profiles are evaluated layer by layer. Clay layers in these models use Matlock curves.
  • The load acts in one horizontal direction; both directions use the same curves.
